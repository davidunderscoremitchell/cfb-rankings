How the rankings work

Metrics breakdown

The Mitchell Metrics reward on-the-field results against real-world teams. Taking both schedule strength (quality of opponents) and game outcomes (wins and losses) into account, the Mitchell Metrics produce a ranking that quantitatively describes what a team has achieved against each individual opponent.

Every team gets three numbers, built in this order:

  1. Strength: how well a team plays, determined from margin of victory/defeat, game location, and opponent Strength. It uses thousands of data points from historical games to judge how hard a team is to beat.
  2. Résumé: the theoretical record that best explains the team's results, given whom it beat and lost to. Résumé is weighted by opponent Strength, as determined above.
  3. PAL ("proven at least"): the low end of the range of theoretical records that reasonably explain a team's results. This is the headline ranking.

Inside the algorithm, each of these numbers is computed as a winning percentage, with .500 corresponding to an average FBS team (6-6 regular season record). On the site, they are displayed as a record. Résumé and PAL are scaled by the number of games played up to that point in the season; Strength is scaled to the typical 12-game season.

The ranking algorithm is described in detail below:

Step 1: The result table

The foundation of the rankings is the result table, which incorporates data from about 7,800 FBS vs. FBS games from 2015 to 2025 (excluding 2020), regular season and bowls. Each game carried the following relevant data:

  • the record disparity between the two teams, using their full-season records with the game itself left out (so a game's outcome doesn't help explain itself);
  • the site of the game (home, away, neutral);
  • the game result: the regulation margin and the outcome (win or loss).

These data points were used to build the result table, which answers the question: "Given the opponents' record disparity and game location, how likely is this outcome and margin?" The result table can be thought of as a "game predictor," against which real game results can be checked to find the likelihood of that outcome. This exact prediction method is used in the next step to determine a team's Strength.

It is worth noting that the score margin is taken at the end of four quarters. Overtime games are thus counted as "0-point margins." The winners and losers of overtime games are still credited with wins and losses, respectively. However, an overtime win is credited as a 0-point win, and an overtime loss as a 0-point loss.

Step 2: Strength

Since the result table can be used to predict game results given various inputs, the same can be done in reverse. In step 2, the algorithm reverse-engineers a team's probable record given game margins, outcomes, and locations. The resulting Strength metric is the record that most realistically produces the results of the actual games. This is the part of the Mitchell Metrics that rewards a team for how it plays, or how hard it is to beat.

Note that the Strength metric does not have to reflect a real record: since the probability of each result is computed game-by-game, the Strength metric finds the record that best reproduces each individual game's outcome. The team detail panel shows this game by game: actual margin vs. the margin expected for a team of that Strength. The algorithm thus computes a team's Strength as the record under which its actual results were most likely.

Strength is an iterative measure that solves all teams at once. Because a team's Strength depends on the Strength of its opponents, Strength must be computed and solved for every team repeatedly until an iteration does not alter the Strength landscape across the sport. This iterative solving across all teams and all games grounds Strength in actual outcomes, and is recalculated with every new result. It measures every team through the network of games actually played, meaning no manual or arbitrary adjustments are needed. This eliminates conference strength metrics, preseason expectations, recruiting rankings, and so on: any relevant metric is already present in the data.

FBS vs. FCS games were proven to be poorly explained by the result table. Because of this, the algorithm for determining Strength cannot be applied to these games. They are therefore not taken into account when determining Strength. FBS vs. FCS games are, however, factored into the Résumé and PAL rankings. This is done by pooling all FCS teams into one "FCS team," which receives a single rating based on all FBS vs. FCS wins and losses, calculated the same way as Résumé.

Step 3: Résumé

Given the Strength rating and game outcome for each opponent a team faced, a theoretical record is again assigned to the team. This record is calculated in a similar fashion as Strength, except this time margins are not considered; only wins and losses count. This is the part of the Mitchell Metrics that rewards outcomes and results.

  • Beating a strong team is evidence of a good team, which increases Résumé.
  • Losing to a strong team is something even good teams do, which marginally decreases Résumé.
  • Losing to a weak team is rare for a good team, which decreases Résumé.
  • Beating a weak team is something even weak teams do, which marginally increases Résumé.
  • Home field advantage is still considered: a road win proves slightly more than a home win.

Historical data indicate that overtime games are close to a coin flip, even between teams of very different Strengths. Therefore, overtime wins and losses are considered less definitive than wins or losses in regulation.

Because the rating of the pooled "FCS team" is so low, beating an FCS school contributes almost nothing to a team's Résumé. Losing to one, however, is catastrophic to a team's Résumé, as the "FCS team" has a significantly lower rating than even the worst FBS team.

Step 4: PAL

A team's results fit a wide range of theoretical records, not just one. While Résumé determines a team's most likely theoretical record, the range of plausible records will encompass many more possibilities. To determine what a team has "proven at least" (PAL), a 95% likelihood interval (every record at least about 15% as likely as the Résumé record) is calculated for each team's Résumé record. PAL is the low end of that range, and is a record that one can confidently say is a team's plausible floor given its results. In other words, PAL = "This team is probably not worse than this." On the other hand, the high end of the 95% likelihood interval can be confidently said to be a team's plausible ceiling given its results.

PAL is chosen as the headline ranking over Résumé for one simple reason: PAL rewards consistency. A team with many consistent results across its games has a narrow likelihood interval because its data all point to one conclusion. A team with inconsistent or "flukey" results has a wide likelihood interval because the data don't all point to the same conclusion. For example, a team with one big upset and otherwise ordinary results may have a high Résumé, but its PAL remains low because one result does not rule out the possibility of being mediocre. Many quality wins, however, make mediocrity increasingly less likely. Early in the season, every team has played only a few games, so all likelihood intervals are wide and PAL values are lower.

Reading the site

  • The table loads sorted by PAL. Click the PAL, Résumé, Strength or Overall header to sort best to worst, and click again for worst to best. Sorting by Overall ranks teams by plain record (win percentage, then wins, then PAL rank). Rank, Change and the top-25 shading follow the active ranking.
  • Click the Change header to sort by each team's movement since last week in the active ranking, from biggest riser to biggest faller; click again to reverse.
  • Ties are broken as follows: PAL ties go to the higher Résumé, then the higher top of the 95% range. Résumé ties go to the higher PAL, then the higher top of the 95% range. Strength ties go to the team with the better 95% likelihood interval computed for Strength. Overall ties go to more wins, then the better PAL rank.
  • Click a team to see its Résumé games (results with each opponent's Strength and PAL rank), its 95% range, and its Strength games (actual vs. expected margin). Click an opponent to jump to it. Use the "Collapse all teams" button above the table to close every open team panel.
  • Conferences are ordered by the average PAL rank of their teams, with their out-of-conference and FCS records alongside. Click a conference for its record against each other conference.
  • Each week's page uses only games through that week.
